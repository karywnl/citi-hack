# Round 1 — submission answers

## 12. Problem

Rural school and college students face three connected barriers to turning education into a livelihood:

1. Limited personalized career guidance: Difficulty exploring careers suited to their interests, strengths, and circumstances, including vocational and non-traditional options.
2. Unclear learning-to-career pathways: Difficulty identifying the skills, courses, qualifications, and entrance exams required to pursue a chosen career.
3. Fragmented access to opportunities: Difficulty finding relevant scholarships, training, apprenticeships, and jobs, understanding eligibility, and navigating applications.

ASER 2023 surveyed 34,745 youth aged 14–18 across 28 rural districts in 26 states. Among surveyed youth, 21.0% did not know or had not thought about their future work aspiration. Among those with a work aspiration, 45.6% reported not knowing anyone doing similar work. These findings highlight gaps in career clarity and exposure, but do not measure every barrier listed above or establish prevalence across the full college-age population. The sample is not nationally representative.

Source: ASER 2023: Beyond Basics, Tables 50–51, pp. 62 and 64; official press release for survey coverage.

## 13. Existing Alternatives

Existing alternatives include:

1. Career guidance: Advice from teachers, family, friends, and school counsellors, alongside career-guidance websites and aptitude tests.
2. Learning pathways: YouTube, coaching centres, college and ITI websites, course platforms, and advice from people already pursuing the chosen career.
3. Opportunity discovery: Scholarship and government portals, apprenticeship and job platforms, school notices, and WhatsApp groups.

## 14. Solution

1. Personalized career exploration: An initial assessment and an observation period involving learner interactions and activities build an evolving profile of interests, demonstrated skills, and circumstances. For college students, this also considers their degree, projects, and existing skills. AI uses this context to recommend and explain career options, refining suggestions as the learner explores further rather than treating the assessment as a fixed verdict on ability.
2. Actionable learning pathways: For a chosen career, provide the required skills, qualifications, courses, and exams, organized into manageable next steps that account for the learner’s current education and skills.
3. Relevant opportunity matching: Match the learner’s profile against verified scholarships, training programmes, apprenticeships, internships, and entry-level jobs, explaining eligibility, deadlines, application steps, and potential ways to interact with prospective employers.

## 15. Key Metrics

1. Career exploration completion: Percentage of new learners who complete the initial assessment and explore a recommended career.
2. Pathway progress: Percentage of learners who complete their next planned learning milestone.
3. Opportunity applications: Percentage of eligible matched learners who submit at least one application.
4. Opportunity conversion: Percentage of applicants who secure a scholarship, training place, apprenticeship, internship, or entry-level job.
5. Monthly active learners: Number of learners taking a meaningful action each month, and its month-on-month growth.

## 16. Unique Value Proposition

Career guidance that grows with the learner combining an initial assessment with ongoing observation to connect each student’s evolving interests and demonstrated skills to actionable learning pathways and relevant livelihood opportunities.

## 17. High-Level Concept

A handy, personalized career counsellor that understands you and grows with you.

## 18. Unfair Advantage

Our team’s personal connections to rural-school educators, a librarian serving rural students, and a staff member at CMC RUHSA offer a potential route to firsthand learner insights and educator feedback. A further connection to a microbiologist who teaches at Malabar Cancer Centre could support review of science and healthcare career pathways. These relationships could help us build context-specific understanding through co-design and pilot testing, subject to willingness, necessary permissions, and participant consent.

## 19. Channels

1. Schools and colleges: Approach rural schools, colleges, teachers, faculty, and placement or career cells to introduce the platform through orientation sessions and supported student onboarding.
2. Libraries and community networks: Reach learners through librarians, community learning spaces, and rural-development organizations.
3. Parent and student groups: Use school- or college-approved WhatsApp groups, student associations, and parent meetings to share information and invite participation.
4. Referrals: Encourage participating students and educators to introduce the platform to peers and other schools and colleges.
5. Institutional outreach: Approach NGOs, corporate CSR programmes, and relevant state- and district-level education and rural-development bodies to explore introductions to schools and colleges, sponsored pilots, and learner outreach.

Initially, we will seek permission to run a small pilot through the team’s existing educator and librarian connections, then expand based on feedback and demonstrated usefulness.

## 20. Customer Segments

1. Primary users: Rural school and college students seeking personalized career guidance, suitable learning pathways, and livelihood opportunities, including students reconsidering their direction or preparing to enter employment.
2. Supporting users: School teachers, college faculty, placement or career-cell staff, librarians, and facilitators who help students explore options and take next steps.
3. Early adopters: Initially, we aim to pilot with Classes 11–12 in rural government schools through existing educator and librarian connections, subject to permission and consent. We would subsequently extend testing to rural college students as access and capacity develop.
4. Paying customers: We propose a mixed model combining institutional funding from schools, colleges, NGOs, or CSR programmes with optional paid features for students or parents, including college students seeking additional guidance. Basic guidance would remain free, while institutions could sponsor access to additional services for underserved learners. Pricing and willingness to pay would be validated through pilots.
